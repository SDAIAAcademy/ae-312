Why the prices differ

Reading a prompt and writing an answer are different work

Prefill

The model reads the whole prompt in one pass. Cheap per token, and paid again on every call.

Decode

The model writes one token at a time, each needing a pass. Expensive per token, and it sets the latency.

Cached prefix

A prompt start the provider has already read is not read again. Billed at a fraction of the input price.

One answer of Anis: 6,831 tokens in, 199 out, SAR 0.0229. The input side is 90% of it, because every turn resends the instructions and the policy text.

Keep this at the level of the bill. How an inference engine implements prefill, decode and its key-value cache is the subject of AE-401; here the three words explain three prices. Ask: for a chat assistant with a long system prompt, which side dominates? The room usually says output. Show the Anis request from Lab 1.1: input is 90% of the cost. That observation comes back in Lab 2.3.

The cost model

A log row holds four kinds of token, and they overlap

uncached = input_tokens - cached_tokens        # input INCLUDES cached
cost = ( uncached          * price["input"]
       + cached_tokens     * price["cached_input"]
       + (output_tokens + reasoning_tokens) * price["output"]   # output EXCLUDES reasoning
       ) / 1e6
What goes wrong. Each provider and each gateway defines these fields its own way. Read the definition before you sum a column: whether input includes cached tokens, and whether output includes reasoning tokens.

The two comments are the whole slide. In this course's log, input includes cached and output excludes reasoning; the rules are written in data/prices.json. Reasoning tokens are tokens the model writes before the answer and that the application never shows: they are billed as output. For the dispute agent they are several times the visible answer.

Retries

A failed call can still be a billed call

Status in the logWhat happenedBilled?

October has 1,751 timeouts in the log. They cost SAR 57 and nobody received their answers.

This is the rule of the course's synthetic provider, written in the price sheet. Real providers differ: check the billing rule for a cancelled or timed-out request with yours. The durable point: the bill follows what the provider did, not what your application received. A cost model that sums successful rows is wrong by exactly the amount that grows during an incident.

Context growth

Every step resends everything before it

step 13,980 tokens in, none cached
step 26,377 tokens in, 3,968 of them cached
step 89,441 tokens in, 9,088 cached
step 7034,823 tokens in, 34,304 cached, after one billed timeout
Cost grows faster than steps. Twice the steps is more than twice the cost, since each step's prompt holds all the earlier ones. In this session half of the cost came after step 44.

The worst session of the month, from Lab 1.2: on 17 October the billing system answered "pending" and the agent asked again for over an hour. The prompt grows by a few hundred tokens per step. Without caching, input grows with the square of the steps. This is what the FinOps Foundation calls context window creep. Sources: FinOps Foundation, GenAI FinOps: how token pricing really works.

Cached tokens, again

An agent loop is affordable because its prefix is cached

The agent's month, as billed
SAR 2,515
The same month with no cached token
SAR 4,339
  • Each step's prompt starts with the previous step's prompt, so most of it is billed at the cached price.
  • Anything that breaks that prefix, such as a new model or an edited history, is paid at the full input price.

From the stretch of Lab 1.2: without its cache the agent's month would cost SAR 4,339; the cache removes 42% of that. Two consequences to plant now. First, switching model tier in the middle of a session throws the cache away (Lab 2.2's stretch prices that). Second, a harness that rewrites or summarises history at every step can cost more than it saves. AE-302 treats compaction as a design choice; here it is a line of the bill.

Retries

A retry resends the whole prompt, and an incident multiplies retries

  • The worst session made 95 attempts for 70 steps; 24 of them were billed timeouts.
  • A client that retries at once, up to four times, turns one provider incident into several times the load.
  • Retries are small in SAR here, 1.5% of the agent's month, and large in waiting: each timeout is a customer who waited a minute.

What to configure

Fewer attempts, a growing pause between them, and a circuit breaker that stops calling a provider that keeps failing.

AE-203 taught timeouts, retries and a fallback model as reliability. Today the same settings are a cost setting. Be honest about the size: on this month retries are the smallest of the four parts. The reason to fix them is mostly the customer, and the capacity an incident eats, not the SAR.

The fix is in the tool

03
Attribution

Allocate first, then optimise

  • One invoice, five teams: until spend has an owner, nobody has a reason to lower it.
  • An owner is a team, an application and an environment.
  • The gateway is where that is recorded, because every request passes through it.

At Mawj

Shurooq in finance receives one PDF a month. The question she is asked is always "whose is it?", and the invoice has no column for that.

The order comes from FinOps practice: the FinOps Foundation's paper on AI says allocate first, then optimise. Optimising before allocating means the cost engineer optimises alone. Ask the room how their organisation answers "whose is it?" today: usually by guessing from the largest team. Sources: FinOps Foundation, FinOps for AI overview.

Two mechanisms

A key says who is calling; a tag says what for

A gateway key

Issued to one team, for one application, in one environment. Attribution costs nothing: it is the join of the log with the key register.

A tag on the request

Sent by the application: app=khulasa;caller=fasil. Finer than a key, and only as reliable as the code that sets it.

Prefer keys. A key cannot be forgotten: a request without one is refused. A tag can be left out, and in October it was on 8% of the shared key's requests.

Maabar's register has eight keys; show data/gateway/keys.csv. Real gateways record spend by key, by user and by team, and accept tags in the request's metadata; LiteLLM's documentation lists spend by tag as an enterprise feature, so check what your gateway gives before designing on tags. Sources: LiteLLM documentation, spend tracking.

A durable rule

Showback before chargeback

1 · Showbackeach team sees its spend; no money moves
→
2 · Corrections"that is not ours": tags and keys get fixed
→
3 · Chargebackbudgets are charged, from a table people trust
Why the order. The first tables are wrong in ways only the teams can see. A team charged from a table it never saw disputes the table, not its spend.

Showback means reporting each team its spend; chargeback means moving the cost into its budget. Plan one or two months of showback. Many organisations never go to chargeback for shared platforms and that can be the right choice: the aim is that teams act on their numbers.

An open question

When one application calls another, there is no settled way to split the cost

Owner pays

Khulasa's team pays for every summary, whoever asked. Simple; the owner carries other teams' demand.

Caller pays

The dispute agent's team pays for the summaries it requests. Fairer on demand; the owner loses a reason to make the service cheaper.

At Mawj the rule moves SAR 46.05 between two teams and changes nothing in the total. State the rule on the table; show what the other one would give.

The entry of this course lists this as unsettled, and it is: FinOps guidance on allocating the cost of agents that call agents is still being written, and it is one of the things to refresh before each cohort. The engineering requirement is the same under either rule: the request must carry who called (here the tag caller=fasil), or no rule can be applied.

Budgets and limits

  • Write soft and hard budgets per application and environment
  • Replay a spike and read which requests the gateway refused

04
Budgets

A soft budget tells someone; a hard budget refuses requests

Soft level

An alert when spend in the window crosses it. Nothing is refused.

Hard level

Every later request in the scope is refused until the window ends.

Window and scope

A day or a month; a key, an application, an environment, a team.

Use both. Soft without hard is a message nobody must act on. Hard without soft is an outage with no warning.

Real gateways have these as settings on keys, teams and users. LiteLLM's names are max_budget, soft_budget and budget_duration, with rpm_limit and tpm_limit for rates, and its documentation states that budgets are enforced against spend read from a database: without one they cap nothing. That is why the lab uses a small in-memory model of a gateway, maabar.py: the concepts are the same and no server is needed. Sources: LiteLLM documentation, budgets and rate limits.

Limits

A rate limit acts in a minute; a budget acts in a day

Rate limit

Requests or tokens per minute. For bursts and loops. At 12 requests a minute, twice the agent's busiest calm minute, the loop was slowed 2 hours 46 minutes before the budget ran out.

Budget

SAR per day or month. For the total. It does not notice a burst until the money is gone.

The cheapest control of all was not at the gateway: a step budget in the agent (Lab 1.2) ends a loop at step 12.

From Lab 1.4 step 5: 857 requests were refused by the rate limit and 349 by the budget. A limit in tokens per minute was tried on this data and refused long-context sessions on a normal morning. Requests per minute fits a loop; tokens per minute fits a batch job. Layer the controls: the agent's own stop rule, then the rate limit, then the budget, then a kill switch a person can use.
