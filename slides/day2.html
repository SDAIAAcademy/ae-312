How much a table can say

With 50 tasks per language, one task is two points

  • 94% and 92% differ by one task. Do not rank on that.
  • A configuration exactly on the floor has passed once, on this sample.

Tiers, cascades and learned routers

Three ways to decide where a request goes

A rule before the call

By something known up front: language, application, intent. Costs nothing. As good as the measurement behind it.

A cascade

Try the cheap tier, check the answer, escalate when the check fails. Pays twice for escalated requests.

A learned router

A small model predicts whether the cheap tier will do. Needs training data and its own evaluation.

Lab 2.2 builds the first two. Whichever you use, it is judged the same way: cost per successful outcome at the floor.

Cascades were studied under the name FrugalGPT; learned routers under RouteLLM, which trains a router between a strong and a weak model from preference data. Both papers report large savings at matched quality on their benchmarks: your tasks decide what you get. Sources: Chen, Zaharia, Zou (2023), FrugalGPT; Ong and others (2024), RouteLLM.

The quality check of a cascade

In production nobody tells the router that an answer was wrong

What a check can see

The call errored. The answer is empty. It is not in the language of the question. It hedges. It does not parse or breaks the schema.

What it cannot see

A fluent answer, in the right language, with the wrong fee. Seeing that needs the right answer, or a grader as good as the large tier.

Measure the check too. On labelled tasks, count the failures it catches, the failures it lets through and the right answers it rejects.

The check is the heart of a cascade and usually the least tested part. A model grader as check is possible and costs a call of its own; then the cascade's arithmetic must include it. In the lab the check is four lines of code and reads only the status, the text and the question.

The number to choose by

Failed first attempts are paid: divide all spend by the successes

spend     = sum(cost of EVERY attempt, the rejected ones too)
successes = number of requests whose final answer was right
cost_per_successful_outcome = spend / successes
Two ways to fool yourself. Counting only the final attempt's cost flatters a cascade. Dividing by requests instead of successes flatters a tier that is cheap and wrong.

The FinOps Foundation's guidance on AI names this unit: measure cost per successful outcome. Day 3 widens "outcome" from a right answer to a resolved dispute, and "cost" to the people who handle what the model could not. Sources: FinOps Foundation, FinOps for AI overview.

Two reasons to call a second model

A fallback answers an outage; an escalation answers a doubt

Fallback

The first model errored or timed out after its retries. The gateway calls another model. Protects availability.

Escalation

The first model answered and the check rejected the answer. The router calls a stronger tier. Protects quality.

  • A fallback target must have passed the same floor, or an outage quietly becomes a quality incident.
  • Both are extra calls: both belong in cost per successful outcome.

Gateways configure fallbacks as a list: after a number of retries a call falls back from one model name to another; LiteLLM's router settings are one example. AE-203 set up a fallback model for reliability; here ask what that fallback was evaluated on. In the lab an errored call of the small tier is treated like a failed check and goes to the medium tier. Sources: LiteLLM documentation, fallbacks.

How a prefix cache works

A prompt is reused only as far as it is identical from its first token

From the start

The cache compares prompts from token 1. The first difference ends the match.

A minimum, in steps

One large provider caches from 1,024 tokens and reports cached tokens in multiples of 128.

For minutes

An unused entry expires after about 5 minutes with two large providers; a use refreshes it. Longer lifetimes exist, at a price.

Prices differ too. With one large provider a cached read costs a tenth of the input price and writing a 5-minute entry costs 1.25 times it.

These are the published rules that the lab's model, M.PrefixCache, takes as parameters: minimum 1,024 tokens, steps of 128, a lifetime of 300 seconds. Providers differ in whether caching is automatic or marked by breakpoints, in lifetimes and in write prices: read the page of yours, and treat every figure here as something to re-verify. How an inference engine keeps and shares this cache is AE-401. Sources: OpenAI documentation, prompt caching; Claude Platform documentation, prompt caching.

Session 4

Semantic caches and their risks

  • Measure hit rate and wrong-answer rate of a semantic cache at several thresholds
  • Scope cache entries so that no answer crosses customers
  • Write the isolation test that proves it
04
The other kind of cache

A response cache skips the model: it serves an answer it gave before

Prefix cache

At the provider. The model still answers; it reads less. Cannot return a wrong answer.

Exact response cache

In your application. The same question, word for word, gets the stored answer.

Semantic response cache

A question that is close enough to a stored one gets its answer. "Close enough" is a threshold on similarity.

A new kind of failure. A response cache can serve an answer that was right for another question, another day or another customer.

The saving is the whole request, so response caches are attractive for a care assistant with repeated questions. The lab's embedding is a small local one, hashed character 3-grams, so that no model or key is needed; it shares the weakness that matters with real embedding models: questions that differ in one decisive word land close together. Entries live in a store the team already runs: a key-value store such as Valkey for exact entries, a vector index such as pgvector for semantic ones.

What a wrong hit looks like

Two questions that differ in the one word that matters

Same device, same city, same wording; one says "no data", the other "error code 31". At threshold 0.80 they are close enough. Plans that differ by a number and "activate" against "deactivate" fail the same way.

The example is the first wrong hit the lab prints at 0.80: a fault report about a landline in Taif, once with no data and once with error code 31. Similarity measures shared wording, and the decisive word is a small part of the wording. Raising the threshold removes most of these, at the price of hits: that trade is the next table.

Isolation between customers

No threshold stops one customer's answer reaching another

  • "What is my current balance?" from two customers is the same question: similarity 1, and an exact match too.
  • The answers differ because the data differs, not the words.
  • 490 answers crossed at every threshold, and with the exact cache.

This is a disclosure

A balance or a bill shown to the wrong person is personal data disclosed. It is an incident to report to the data protection officer, not a quality metric.

The course's responsible-use statement says it plainly: a semantic cache must never serve one customer's answer to another. At Mawj, Yaqoub would treat each of the 490 as a disclosure of personal data under the organisation's obligations; the national references are the Personal Data Protection Law and its implementing regulation, cited in the course sources. Keep the legal reading to him: the engineering duty is that it cannot happen.

Lab 2.4 · the fix and its test

Scope every entry by whose data produced it, and test that nothing crosses

def scope_of(q):                      # known when the answer is written
    return f"customer:{q['customer']}" if q["used_customer_data"] else "public"

def test_no_answer_crosses_customers(served):
    assert served.crossed.sum() == 0
  • An answer that read the customer's account lives in that customer's partition; everything else is shared.
  • The test needs no labels. It fails on the unscoped cache and passes on the scoped one, at every threshold.

The application knows, when it writes an answer, whether producing it called a tool that read the account: that flag, not a guess from the wording, decides the partition. Many teams go further and do not cache personal answers at all. Either way the test belongs in the pipeline: it is the passing cross-customer isolation test the outcome asks for.

Staleness

A cached answer is right until the world changes

  • On day 15 the price of one plan changed. At 0.95 the cache served the old price twice, on days 23 and 25.
  • A 3-day lifetime on every entry removes both, and cuts the hit rate from 25.6% to 9.0%.
  • Better: tie entries to the version of what they were built from, and drop them when it changes.

Three questions for any cache

Whose data is in the entry? How long is it true? What tells the cache that it no longer is?

From the stretch of Lab 2.4. Two stale answers in a month sounds small; each one is a customer told a price that Mawj no longer offers. A lifetime is the blunt tool and costs most of the saving here, because questions repeat over weeks. Invalidation by version of the plan sheet keeps the hit rate and needs the application to know what an answer depended on.
